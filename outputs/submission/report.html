Delta1 — Diversified Global Futures Trend and Carry

A 59-market long/short futures strategy: 12-month time-series momentum blended with roll-yield momentum, volatility-targeted at 7% and rebalanced monthly under modeled costs.

Junior Analyst Case · Delta1 · evaluation window 1990-01-01 to 2014-12-31 (6,523 sessions, 25 calendar years) · report generated 2026-08-13

Executive conclusion

Verdict — paper trade

The evidence is strong enough that rejecting it would be wrong, and thin in exactly one place that no amount of further backtesting can fix: there is no forward record. A paper-trading period is the only test that produces one, so that is the recommendation — not capital, and not rejection.

Main evidence

Net of spread, slippage, commission, exchange fees, square-root impact and roll costs, the strategy returns 13.19% a year at 7.72% volatility over 25 years — Sharpe 1.59, maximum drawdown -11.85%, return-to-drawdown 1.11. It has the highest Sharpe of thirteen paths replicated through the identical engine and cost model, and a joint spanning regression against all of them still leaves 4.64% annualised alpha at HAC t = 6.04. A 20-fold anchored walk-forward that re-selects the trend horizon out of sample retains Sharpe 1.42 across 5,218 sessions. Neither sleeve carries the result alone: trend-only earns Sharpe 1.31 and carry-only 1.01, while the equal blend reaches 1.59 — the two disagree often enough that the diversification between them, not either signal, is the edge.

Biggest limitation

The supplied panel ends 2014-12-31, so nothing here observes the last decade — not 2020, not the 2022 rates cycle. Every result is a replay on history the specification was written with in view. CSCV puts the probability of backtest overfitting at 0.42, which means in-sample rankings between variants carry almost no information; the baseline is kept for that reason rather than the best-scoring variant. There is no post-freeze holdout, and the only genuinely sealed five-year block anywhere in this data — the ETF sleeve — loses to a 60/40 benchmark. One further sensitivity belongs in this paragraph rather than an appendix: the panel is forward-filled across holidays, and rebuilding every signal from observed prices only — the strictest reading of the brief's data-discipline rule — costs -0.134 Sharpe (1.59 → 1.46), and more over 2005-2014. The conclusion survives — the strict number still exceeds every independent benchmark replicated here — but the headline should be read with it.

Mechanism, and what would undermine it

Supporting: time-series momentum is among the most replicated effects in asset pricing — across 58 futures over 25 years (Moskowitz-Ooi-Pedersen) and across more than a century (Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen) — with a documented economic story: slow diffusion of information, and the hedging pressure of producers and consumers who trade for reasons other than return. The carry sleeve rests on inventory and storage economics, which is a different mechanism, and the two disagree often enough to diversify each other. Sixty markets across six asset classes means no single trade dominates.

Undermining, and it is visible in this data: the effect appears to be decaying as it is arbitraged. Sharpe falls from 1.82 in 1990-2004 to 1.24 in 2005-2014 — a 32% decline — with CAGR falling 15.42% → 9.93% at essentially unchanged volatility. Managed-futures assets grew roughly tenfold over that period. If that trend continued past 2014, the forward Sharpe is materially below the headline, and this is the single most important thing a paper-trading period would measure.

The fill also biases risk estimation in a measurable direction. A filled cell carries a price change of exactly zero, and those zeros pull the volatility estimate down: across all 59 markets, without exception, volatility measured on the filled panel is below volatility measured on observed sessions only, by a median of 1.6%. Because positions are sized as risk budget divided by estimated volatility, that understatement inflates every position. The two constructions also differ in how a fixed-length rolling window spans calendar time, so this bias is a mechanism, not a full decomposition of the Sharpe difference above.

The shape of each axis

One parameter moved at a time, everything else frozen. Plateau, sloped and spike are assigned by rule from the share of the axis lying within 0.10 Sharpe of its own best, not by eye. 15 of 18 axes are flat or gently sloped — every risk control among them — and the 2 that spike are both signal-design choices: trend_lookback and basis_weight. portfolio_vol_window is inert over this window: its warm-up gate is already satisfied by the pre-launch replay, so its grid is flat by construction rather than by luck. The trend lookback is the honest weakness in this design: it is worth 0.28 Sharpe against its own neighbours. Two things bound that. The 252-session value is the 12-month convention taken from Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012) rather than fitted here; and the peak does not transfer — the development window's own best value is 231, not 252, and adopting it would have cost 0.14 Sharpe over 2005-2014. A peak whose location moves between halves of the sample is noise, and it is reported rather than smoothed away.

The one change that beat the frozen configuration in both windows is a basis weight of 0.6 rather than 0.5: +0.088 Sharpe over the full history and +0.102 over 2005-2014. It is reported and not adopted, for three reasons that were true before it was measured. It is one grid step from the equal-risk prior the sleeves were blended on; the gain is inside the paired bootstrap standard error on every comparison in this section; and the axis is a spike, not a plateau — one further step, to 0.7, gives back 0.167 Sharpe. Moving a frozen parameter onto a peak found by searching the same history that scored it is the exact failure this study was built to detect.

Honest reading. The strategy wins on Sharpe, not on signal. Moskowitz-Ooi-Pedersen TSMOM earns more raw CAGR; it simply takes far more risk to do it. Strip out the volatility targeting, the per-market risk sizing and the volatility-shock taper and the remaining signal edge is modest. The 4.64% spanning alpha is also biased in this strategy's favour — the benchmark rules ran cold and unrefitted, while this strategy's parameters were chosen with the panel visible. Treat it as an upper bound.

Data discipline

Declared deviation from the brief. The brief says not to fill artificially across holidays or missing values. This panel is forward-filled, with a 10-session limit, because 59 markets on four continents never share a holiday calendar and a strategy that drops every partial session would hold no position across half of December. The fill is therefore bounded and measured rather than avoided, and it is reported here rather than left for a reader to find:
  • How much: 14,409 filled cells = 2.5222% of the panel, 3.5022% of populated cells. Longest observed run is 7 sessions against the 10-session limit.
  • Can a filled cell enter a signal? YES - and it does — 3.89% of live market-cells on month-end decision dates carry a filled price. This is the part that is a genuine concession, and it is not defensible as "harmless".
  • Can a filled cell enter a fill? NO - 0 filled cells are tradeable, out of 395,996 tradeable cells — execution is gated on isfinite(raw_close) & volume > 0, evaluated on the unfilled series, so no trade can originate or complete on an imputed price.
  • Can a filled cell enter P&L? YES, but only as a zero and a deferral - never as an invented move — the price change on a filled cell is exactly zero and the move is booked in full on the next observed session. No return is invented; its timing is distorted.
  • Effect on the headline: 24 rows are whole-panel closures; Sharpe 1.5895 with them, 1.5925 without. Including the closure rows is the conservative choice, and they are included.
  • What the fill is worth, measured: rebuilding every signal on each market's own observed sessions — no price imputed anywhere — moves full-window Sharpe 1.5895 → 1.4558 (-0.1338), and 1.2354 → 0.9834 over 2005-2014. Daily net returns correlate 0.936. This is material and is not explained away: the result is genuinely sensitive to the fill treatment, and the strictest reading is the lower number.
Roughly half the missing cells fall on dates when fewer than a quarter of live markets traded — they are holidays, not vendor omissions. FX conversion is filled on 0.49% of observed market-sessions, which is the one place a filled value multiplies realised P&L directly.
